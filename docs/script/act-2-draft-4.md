## What Act II is for

Act I spent one close look on one drawing. Act II shows where a close look pays most across many pieces
of work: the few things at the centre that every agent reads, because a rule that is wrong but agrees
with everything built from it gets copied faster than a person can find the copies, and the only fix is
at the source.

**From Act I:** the correct bike is riding when Act II opens; its particles gather into the first piece
of work, one small body with its satellites far out on an arm, and the camera pulls away until it is one
of hundreds. **To the Coda:** the last line, "the closer to the centre a thing sits, the longer you look
at it", is the Coda's map said in one sentence; the map is rings around a centre.

## Words

The prose names things by what they are, not by how the picture draws them (author, 2026-10-05): rules,
plans, code, a defect, a reviewer. Where a thing sits is said in words that are true of the picture
without naming it: the centre, around them, out at the edge. The picture keeps its own vocabulary in the
stage directions only: the hub, the ring and the arms; bodies and the satellites that go round them;
streams; and the stutter that shows a defect up close. The prose never says galaxy, planet, moon, atom
or orbit; the reader may see any of them.

## Labels on the regions

**No labels** (the author, 2026-10-06). Pages 2 to 4 name the three regions in the lines, one scene
each, in the order the camera visits them: in to the centre first, then out with the streams; after
that the shapes carry it, and later lines say "at the centre", "out here".

## Choices I made

- **II.1 (zoom out):** the pull-away is now the powers of ten the author asked for (one piece, its arm,
  all of it), straight up, with the bike's particles gathering into the first piece as the other pieces
  come up. No line names the scales; the picture does them.
- **II.2 (the regions):** three regions, one scene each (pages 2–4), in the order rules → plans → code,
  which is the lineage's order: the camera flies in to the centre and then rides a stream out, so the
  tour travels the way the streams flow. Then a wide shot of how they connect (page 5). Pace layering is
  seen (big and slow at the centre, small and quick at the edge), not said.
- **The defect is a behaviour, not a shape.** A piece that derps is a piece whose motion is wrong once
  in a while; that is the essay's premise (mechanical systems whose motion reveals whether they work)
  and it is why the defect is invisible from far away and needs watching up close. The prose says
  "defect" and "watch"; the picture decides whether it is a satellite or the body that stutters, and
  how often.
- **II.4 (overload) cut; II.5 (promotion)** survives only as the inward stream on page 9, told as the
  risk; **II.6 (meta)** is cut, its one sentence is the last line. (As draft 3.)
- **Colour:** no colour or dot words anywhere. Who did what is carried by the verbs and by distance.
- **The tally** ("twelve new defects") is in page 8's last line; the storyboard must show exactly twelve.
- **No fence** at the source's outflow (the author, 2026-10-06).

### The thesis

"The higher up a thing sits, the longer you look at it" carried hierarchy ("higher-ups", "top-down").
The galaxy carries something better: a centre that everything else is built around and that the whole
picture turns on. Three versions were tried against "lands as well or better":

1. **"The closer to the centre a thing sits, the longer you look at it."** (14) Chosen. It keeps the
   author's reviewed frame (*the X-er a thing sits, the longer you look at it*) and changes only the
   gradient; it is literally the overhead picture on page 15; and it makes the Coda's map a thing you can
   draw (rings around a centre, the inner rings hottest), where "higher up" needed a stair. It also rhymes
   with the act's method: "Come closer" and "Up close" are how you find a defect, "closer to the centre" is
   where you look for one before it exists.
2. *"The more that turns around a thing, the longer you look at it."* (13) Says *why* (leverage), and is
   true of the picture if the whole turns on the hub. But it is a quantity, not a place, so it is harder to
   draw as the Coda's map; and "turns around" is one step less plain for a child.
3. *Two lines:* "Everything turns around the rules." / "The closer to the centre a thing sits, the longer
   you look at it." The first line is the galaxy saying leverage out loud. It is the strongest version to
   read aloud and the one that breaks the "last page keeps only the lesson" decision; and the Coda needs
   one sentence, not two.

Offered as open choice 1.

### Where "system" lands

**Removed by the author (2026-10-09):** "system" is ambiguous here. ly-6 is deleted and the word is
not moved: Act II's narration never says it. What follows is the record of the placements weighed.

The word should land once (twice at most) and be true of the picture when it does. Three placements:

- **A. The zoom-out as powers of ten (page 1).** "One piece of work. The system around it. All of it."
  Rejected: it adds a line to the page the author trimmed to two, and the middle power of ten has no
  name the prose ever uses again; the pull-back under zo-2 does the powers of ten wordlessly.
- **B. The moment rules, plans and code are seen as one thing (page 5).** A new line after "Rules
  shape plans, and plans shape code": **"All of it is one system."** Chosen. It is the first frame that
  holds everything, and the word names what the reader has just watched assemble out of three scenes;
  it is true at every scale the picture offers (a body with its satellites is a system, the whole is a
  system); and it plants the word the act's ending relies on, since "fix the source" is "fix the system
  at its centre", without the thesis having to carry the word. As its own beat it gets its own fade and
  lands as a moment rather than as a clause.
- **C. The wide shot (page 6).** "From far away, **the whole system** looks right." A one-word change on
  a reviewed line, and thematically exact (at system scale the system looks fine; that is the trap).
  Second choice: take it instead of B if the author would rather not add a line. Not as well as B,
  because B and C are consecutive lines, so it is one or the other.

Rejected for the thesis: every version with "system" in it ("The closer to the centre of the system a
thing sits…") is longer and flatter. Rejected for up-2: "as fast as the system makes them" reads as
blaming the machine, and the author's "as they are made" is tighter.
